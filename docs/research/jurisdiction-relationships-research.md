# Jurisdiction relationships — research notes

Working notes behind the Wave 4 cluster. The comparative model, the country evidence matrix,
and the distinctions the cluster exists to keep apart.

---

## 1. "Jurisdiction" is at least six things

The single largest source of confusion in this area is one word doing several jobs. The
cluster separates them explicitly and never uses them interchangeably:

| Concept                           | Question it answers                                                              |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Territorial jurisdiction**      | Where may this agency act?                                                       |
| **Subject-matter jurisdiction**   | What may it act on?                                                              |
| **Legal competence**              | Which instrument creates the power — constitution, statute, statute of autonomy? |
| **Administrative responsibility** | Who funds, staffs and directs the organisation?                                  |
| **Operational responsibility**    | Who actually attends and does the work?                                          |
| **Command authority**             | Who may give an order that must be obeyed?                                       |

Three further relationships sit on top of these and are covered on their own pages:
**coordination** (alignment without authority), **contract service** (delivery bought from
another order of government), and **shared service** (delivery run jointly between peers).

The practical payoff is that several familiar confusions dissolve once the terms are apart:

- A federal agency may hold nationwide subject-matter jurisdiction and no command authority
  over anyone.
- A province may hold administrative responsibility while another order of government holds
  operational responsibility — contract policing.
- A national body may set standards adopted by forces it cannot direct.

## 3. Counterexamples, and why each page needs one

A comparative page's characteristic failure is a reader taking the pattern for a rule. Two
implications are wrong and both are natural:

- **"Federal country" → "sub-national police."** Nigeria is a federation of 36 states whose
  constitution forbids any police force other than the national one.
- **"Municipality" → "municipal police force."** Japanese municipalities do not own ordinary
  police forces; policing is prefectural.

| Page                                   | Counterexample | Establishes                                                                                                                                     |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| How policing is divided between levels | Nigeria, Kenya | Federation and devolution both fail to imply sub-national policing                                                                              |
| Contract policing                      | Switzerland    | Shared is not contracted — cooperation between peers, no provider and no bill                                                                   |
| Command and coordination               | France         | Where a system genuinely _is_ hierarchical, command does run downwards — so hierarchy must be established per system, not assumed absent either |
| Municipal and national police          | Japan          | Municipality does not imply municipal police                                                                                                    |
| Local police governance                | Spain          | "Local control" can be three simultaneous relationships in one territory                                                                        |
| Sheriffs and city police               | France         | The office does not travel; no elected county-level law-enforcement office exists there                                                         |

The France counterexample on the command page is the one worth noting: most of this cluster
argues that cooperation is _not_ hierarchy, and the counterexample guards against the
opposite over-correction — concluding that police hierarchy never exists.

## 4. Three distinctions the cluster exists to protect

### Command ≠ coordination

Cooperation between agencies is routinely read as hierarchy. It usually is not, and the
difference decides who is answerable: under coordination each force remains responsible for
its own decisions; under command, responsibility travels up the chain.

Evidence: Japan's national organisation supervises and coordinates while prefectures
administer; Switzerland's federal office states it is not superordinate. Standard-setting,
information sharing, mutual assistance and temporary joint-operation command structures are
each described separately, because each confers a different (usually zero) amount of
authority.

### Contract ≠ ownership

A jurisdiction that contracts policing does not acquire the provider force. Three things stay
apart throughout: who holds the **responsibility**, who **owns** the institution, who
**delivers** the service. The clearest evidence is that the RCMP maintains its own national
standards inside contract jurisdictions — the Force stays federal while delivering provincial
and municipal policing.

### Contracted ≠ shared

A shared service is run jointly between peers; a contracted service has a provider, a client
and a bill. Switzerland is the shared case: no national force, cantonal policing under
cantonal law, a federal office working alongside rather than above.

## 5. What was deliberately not asserted

- That any allocation of policing is better than another. The levels page says so in its
  `uncertainty` field.
- That national coordination bodies command operational policing.
- That all sheriffs run jails, provide court security, or are elected — the cited source
  explicitly does not support these, and the page carries that limitation verbatim.
- That every autonomous community, province or state within one country is arranged the same
  way. Canada and Spain are both asymmetric and both say so.
- Any staffing figure. The US source carries one; it was not used, because staffing is a
  restricted-claim category and the argument does not need it.
