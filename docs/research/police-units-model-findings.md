# Police units, vehicles and organisational structure — model findings

Research findings from Knowledge Expansion Wave 27. Four guides, four institutional sources, two
services reached: New Zealand Police and the Netherlands' Politie.

This is a **structure** wave. It answers where a capability sits and how far it reaches, not what
it can do. That boundary is the whole design and it is enforced by tests rather than by intent.

## What the research actually found

### "Unit" is a structural word, and reading it as an operational one misreads most of the list

New Zealand Police publishes its teams and units alongside its districts. The published list holds
a Dive Squad and Armed Offenders Squads — and also the Royal New Zealand Police College, a Police
Prosecution Service, Communications Centres and a Financial Crime Group.

A college is not a squad. A prosecution service is not a squad. They appear in the same list because
"unit" describes **where something sits in a structure**, not what kind of work it does. The popular
reading — a unit is a team of specialists who do one operational job — fails against the majority of
the service's own list.

This is the wave's central finding and it is what separates the page from Wave 24's
`specialist-roles-in-policing`. That page owns _what specialisations exist_, comprehensively, across
ten named areas of work. This one owns _what an organisational body is_ — a distinct question that
the corpus had never asked.

### A unit has a reach, and the reach is the least obvious property

The single most useful thing a published structure discloses is not the unit's name. It is the
sentence about geography attached to it. One service publishes **four different arrangements at
once**:

| Arrangement                        | As published                                                                                                  |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Distributed                        | Police Dog Section — **21 sections nationwide**                                                               |
| Concentrated in a few places       | Maritime Units — **Auckland and Wellington**                                                                  |
| Single-sited, nationally available | Dive Squad — "based in Wellington, but responds to requests for assistance from all over New Zealand"         |
| Locally based, national option     | Air support — "usually operates throughout the metropolitan Auckland area", "may also be deployed nationally" |

A reader who concludes "the police have a dive squad, so there is one near me" is wrong in precisely
the way the published wording is careful to avoid. The service is not being evasive; it is being
exact, and the exactness is only visible when the four statements are read side by side.

### "Responds to requests for assistance" is a coordination relationship, not a command one

The phrasing is load-bearing. A district cannot direct the Dive Squad — it asks, and somebody
decides. That is structurally the same problem the corpus already describes _between_ separate
agencies at `police-command-and-coordination`, appearing here _inside_ a single service.

### Distribution reflects capability maintenance, not importance

Specialist capability is expensive and perishable: it needs enough work to keep people practised.
Outside dense areas there often is not enough. The honest reading of uneven distribution is usually
this constraint rather than neglect — and a published structure is what lets the public ask the
question at all.

### The Dutch source describes vehicles by category, and the categories are ordinary

Politie publishes its vehicle categories. The ordinary car and van come **first**, used for patrol,
responding to emergency calls and traffic checks. The specialised categories follow, including a
`trainingsvoertuig` — a training vehicle, which is not an operational category at all.

The same imbalance Wave 24 found in measured academy hours and Wave 26 found in issued equipment
lists appears a third time: **what a service actually maintains is a better guide to the job than
what fiction selects from it.**

### Police livery is legally protected, and the reason is public safety

The Dutch police house style is `wettelijk beschermd` — legally protected. The stated reason is not
brand management: it is that a member of the public must be able to trust that a marked vehicle is
what it appears to be. Recognisability is an accountability and safety function, which is the same
conclusion Wave 26 reached about the uniform.

## What was deliberately not used

The Politie vehicles page **also** describes operational uses of a rapid-intervention vehicle,
including pursuit and interception. That material is researched, available, and deliberately unused.

It describes **capability**. This wave describes **category**. The distinction is recorded in the
source note itself (`nl-politie-voertuigen`) so that a later editor meets the decision rather than
rediscovering the paragraph and assuming it was missed.

Nothing in this wave describes: what any unit can do; response times; staffing; specifications;
performance; ANPR or other detection capability; pursuit or interception technique; what any marking
looks like in enough detail to reproduce; or how to obtain any of it.
