# Where a competence guarantee attaches — model findings

Research findings from Knowledge Expansion Wave 29, and the closing argument of the professions arc
that began in Wave 24.

Four guides, three new sources, two reused, three systems: England and Wales, the Netherlands and
New Zealand.

## The finding that makes it an arc rather than a list

Asking the same question of eight professions produced three different answers, and the difference
is architectural rather than a matter of rigour.

**The guarantee attaches to the person.** Lawyers are admitted by a regulator that can withdraw the
admission; judges are appointed or enter a corps. The authority is general — it covers a field, it
is granted once, and it travels with the individual.

**The guarantee attaches to the unit and the activity.** In forensic science, accreditation is
granted to a _forensic unit_ against named international standards, for named activities, recorded
on a schedule. The individual's competence is defined, demonstrated and tested inside that
arrangement. There is no admission to a profession called "forensic scientist".

**The guarantee attaches to the employing service.** In emergency call handling, the service trains
the person itself after appointment. There is no external qualification to acquire first, which is
why what is asked for on entry can be short.

None of the three is the serious one. They are answers to differently shaped problems: a general
professional authority suits work that is one field; an activity-specific scheme suits work that
divides into many techniques with little transfer between them; and internal training suits work no
external body teaches.

## Forensic science: what accreditation actually covers

This filled a gap the corpus had declared against itself. `/forensics/who-regulates-forensic-science`
says in its own uncertainty that "accreditation standards themselves — what a laboratory must
demonstrate to be accredited — were not researched and are not described", and it cites neither UKAS
nor any ISO standard.

- **One assessing body, three standards.** The Regulator recognises UKAS as the sole national
  accreditation body, and "Where the Code requires accreditation, UKAS will assess forensic units
  undertaking FSAs against ISO/IEC 17025:2017, ISO/IEC 17020:2012 or BS EN ISO 15189:2022." Three
  standards rather than one, because testing, inspection and medical laboratory work are not the
  same kind of activity.
- **A single person can be a unit.** The code defines a forensic unit as "a legal entity or part of
  a legal entity that performs any part of an FSA" and states that it "can be a team, a unit or a
  single practitioner". The framework therefore does not depend on an organisation being large, and
  an expert working alone is inside it rather than outside.
- **Scope is the point.** Compliance is demonstrated "by having accreditation to the standard, the
  Code and the sub-activities of the FSA that the organisation undertakes, on the schedule of
  accreditation". Reading "accredited" without reading the schedule tells you very little.
- **Not everything requires it.** Accreditation is required for most listed activities but not all,
  and some have other compliance routes. "All forensic work must be accredited" is the most likely
  wrong summary of this material, and a mutation proof confirmed it was the one the suite did not
  yet catch.

## Forensic science: where the individual actually appears

- A practitioner is "any individual who is directly involved in undertaking an FSA" — the obligation
  reaches people by what they do, not by a title or a past examination.
- Quality management includes "defining, demonstrating and testing the initial and ongoing
  competence of personnel". Three verbs and two time-points, all named. A scheme with only the first
  is a policy; with only the second, an induction.
- "All practitioners are required to declare their compliance to the Code via a declaration in their
  reports." This is where an organisational framework becomes an individual statement, and where it
  can later be examined.

**What the code does not say** is what anyone needs in order to enter the field. It states no entry
qualification, and none is claimed.

## Emergency call handling: short entry conditions, and one candid requirement

- The Dutch national police state a **level rather than a subject** — "mbo 3 werk- en denkniveau" —
  and the training is explicitly their own: "een interne opleiding tot centralist meldkamer (die je
  van ons krijgt)".
- The same page asks for "stressbestendigheid en mentale weerbaarheid (vanwege de heftige situaties
  die je kunt meemaken)". The parenthesis does real work: the service names _why_ in the same
  sentence. Most published criteria describe what a person must be able to do; this one describes
  what they must be able to withstand, and says what they will meet.
- New Zealand Police state **no academic requirement at all** for either communicator role, and put
  "The first six weeks of your employment will be fulltime training" at the start, on a roster that
  "covers 24 hours a day, 365 days including public holidays and weekends".

## The work divides differently in different services

New Zealand separates the roles and names the handover: "Communicators find out what's happening and
where, decide on the right police response and send information through to the police dispatchers
who are responsible for getting police units on their way." Note where the decision sits — with the
role that speaks to the caller.

The Netherlands names both halves as one task: "je neemt de spoedeisende meldingen (112) aan en
stuurt de eenheden op straat aan."

Both are accurate descriptions of their own service. Read together, they show that "the person who
answers emergency calls" is not one occupation, and that assuming it is produces a wrong picture of
at least one of them. Separating the roles creates a handover, where information can be lost;
combining them divides attention. Neither arrangement removes the difficulty, and nothing researched
here says either performs better.

## What was deliberately not used

- **The United States is not described.** 911.gov returned HTTP 403 to every automated request
  across three paths. That is an access limitation and never evidence about American arrangements.
- **A membership association was excluded as a source.** The obvious reference for dispatcher
  training standards is a professional association that also sells courses. Citing it would have
  put a commercial training seller into the registry, and no existing corpus-wide guard would have
  caught it, since such a host is neither a retailer nor obviously a teaching site. A new guard now
  covers it.
- **No count of New Zealand communications centres is published.** The page names four locations
  while also stating a total that does not match them. Publishing either number would publish a
  number the page does not support.
- **UKAS's own pages could not be retrieved**, so its role rests on the statutory code's statement
  of it rather than its own.
