# Wave 7 — global police-oversight function matrix

Rows are researched bodies. Columns are functions, not names. Values are `YES`, `NO`,
`CONDITIONAL`, `PARTIAL` and `NOT ESTABLISHED`.

**`NOT ESTABLISHED` is never converted into `NO`.** It means the wave did not reach a Tier-1
source for that cell. A reader and a test can both tell the difference between "the instrument
says it cannot" and "we did not establish it".

## Legend

| Value             | Meaning                                                                     |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `YES`             | The instrument gives the body this function                                 |
| `NO`              | The instrument withholds it, or expressly assigns it elsewhere              |
| `CONDITIONAL`     | Available only on a trigger — a request, an instruction, a defined category |
| `PARTIAL`         | Held over part of the field only                                            |
| `NOT ESTABLISHED` | Not reached from a Tier-1 source in this wave                               |

## D. What the matrix forces

Reading down the columns rather than across the rows produces four findings that no single
country establishes.

**1. Complaint intake and criminal investigation are separable, and are separated.**
Norway's chief **must reject** a report that discloses no offence. Czechia's Act contains no
complaint procedure for other forces at all — only alerts about _criminal_ conduct. Both bodies
investigate crimes by officers and neither handles conduct complaints. Meanwhile Ireland's PCSA
inspects and never investigates, and Ireland's Fiosrú investigates and never inspects. Four
bodies, four different subsets of the same column set.

**2. Almost nothing on this list disciplines.**
Across sixteen current bodies, the disciplinary-decision column is `NO` or `CONDITIONAL`
everywhere. The single `CONDITIONAL` is Kenya's **internal** unit, and only where the
Inspector-General authorises it. The strongest duty found anywhere is South Africa's § 30, which
compels the Commissioner to _initiate_ proceedings on the Directorate's recommendation — a duty
on the police to act, not a power in the oversight body to decide.

**3. Prosecution is the rare power, and it is not held by the civilian bodies.**
One body in the set prosecutes: Norway's Spesialenheten. Sweden's prosecuting power sits in the
prosecution service. Every civilian oversight body researched refers instead — to the NPA, the
DPP, the státní zástupce or the procureur.

**4. Position does not predict independence, in either direction.**
Sweden's SU is described in law as an _independent department inside_ the Police Authority.
Kenya's Internal Affairs Unit is statutorily insulated from police command while reporting to
the Inspector-General. France's external Défenseur des droits cannot sanction while its internal
IGPN opens criminal investigations on its own initiative. South Africa's § 4(1) states
independence **from the Service** and says nothing about the Minister, who nominates and may
remove the Executive Director. Any page that treats "external" as a synonym for "independent"
would be wrong about four of the sixteen bodies here.
