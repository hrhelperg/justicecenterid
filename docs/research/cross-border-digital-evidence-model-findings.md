# Cross-border digital evidence — model findings

Wave 23. Three international and supranational instruments and two United States sections, read
on 5 September 2026. Everything below is a statement about the instruments' own text. No national
implementing legislation and no case law was read for any country, and nothing here establishes
what any State has enacted.

## 1. The finding the wave was built on

**The instruments do not share a connecting factor.** Three mechanisms answer "which legal system
connects this authority to this data", and each answers with a different fact about the world.

| Instrument                | What connects the authority to the data                                                         | Where the text says so |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Convention on Cybercrime  | The **territory** in which the data is stored                                                   | Arts. 29, 31           |
| Regulation (EU) 2023/1543 | Where the **addressee** is established or has a legal representative                            | Art. 3(1)              |
| 18 U.S.C. § 2713          | What the **provider** has in its possession, custody or control — location expressly irrelevant | § 2713                 |

The practical consequence is that the same set of facts can produce three different answers about
which mechanism is available, and a page written as though one test governed would be wrong about
two instruments. This is the single most load-bearing fact in the wave, and every page rests on it.

## 2. A treaty in force can still build nothing

Article 25(2) of the Convention provides that "Each Party shall also adopt such legislative and
other measures as may be necessary to carry out the obligations set forth in Articles 27 through
35". Chapter III is a construction plan, stated as such inside the instrument. A Party bound by the
Convention has undertaken to build the machinery; whether it has is a separate question about that
Party, and one this wave did not research for any Party.

Every operative article of the Second Additional Protocol opens with the same formula.

## 3. Four instruments, four lifecycle positions

Stated as at 5 September 2026, and only as at that date.

| Instrument                 | Position                                                                               |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Convention on Cybercrime   | In force; its cooperation articles require Parties to legislate (Art. 25(2))           |
| Second Additional Protocol | Concluded 12 May 2022; the register consulted leaves its entry-into-force column empty |
| Regulation (EU) 2023/1543  | Directly applicable, applying from 18 August 2026 (Art. 34(2))                         |
| Directive (EU) 2023/1544   | Transposition due by 18 February 2026 (Art. 7(1)); completion not researched           |

Two refinements matter more than the table. First, one instrument carries two dates: Article 34(2)
makes the Regulation applicable from 18 August 2026 and, in the same paragraph, makes the Article
19 decentralised IT system obligation applicable one year after implementing acts adopted under
Article 25 — and whether those acts have been adopted was not researched, so no date is stated for
it. Second, the pairing is load-bearing: the Regulation addresses an addressee that the Directive
is what obliges providers to have, so the applicable instrument depends on the transposed one.

### On the Protocol's status, stated exactly

The official treaty database of the Government of the Netherlands, on a page stating that its
information is valid on 5 September 2026, records a single lifecycle entry for the Protocol — its
conclusion, dated 12 May 2022 — and leaves the entry-into-force column empty. That establishes what
that register records on that date. It is **not** a claim that the Protocol has no entry-into-force
date anywhere.

**Party lists and ratification counts were NOT RESEARCHED.** The Council of Europe Treaty Office is
the authoritative register for both and returned HTTP 403 to three separate URL forms. Search-engine
summaries giving a count were deliberately not used, and no count appears anywhere in the wave.

## 4. Dual criminality enters at one step and not the other

Article 29(3) provides that for expedited preservation "dual criminality shall not be required as a
condition to providing such preservation". Article 29(4) then permits a Party to reserve the right
to refuse the request for preservation in defined cases where it foresees that dual criminality
cannot be fulfilled as a condition for disclosure.

The design separates the two steps and prices them differently: freezing carries the lighter
condition, and disclosure is where the requested State's own criminal law re-enters. That is the
same preservation/production distinction Wave 22 found inside domestic codes, appearing again at the
border with a different variable attached.

## 5. Article 32 admits exactly two situations

Its heading is "Trans-border access to stored computer data with consent or where publicly
available", and the article's two limbs are publicly available data, and data accessed with the
"lawful and voluntary consent of the person who has the lawful authority to disclose it". There is
no third limb. An instrument that is often described as authorising cross-border access authorises
it in two named situations and no others.

## 6. The Protocol's two routes reach different categories

| Route                                                | Reaches                                                       |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Art. 7 — order direct to a provider in another Party | Specified, stored **subscriber information** only             |
| Art. 8 — through the requested Party                 | Specified, stored **subscriber information and traffic data** |

Content data appears in neither. The categories are what separate a direct route from one that runs
through the other Party — which makes the category question (§8 below) structural rather than
definitional.

And the safeguard on the direct route is **optional in form**: Article 7(2)(b) allows a Party to
_declare_ that orders to providers in its territory must be issued by, or under the supervision of,
a prosecutor or other judicial authority. It is a declaration a Party may make, not a default the
Protocol sets.

## 7. The EU authorisation ladder turns on the category _and_ on the act

Article 4 of Regulation (EU) 2023/1543, verified against its own text:

- **4(1)** — production order for subscriber data, or for data requested for the sole purpose of
  identifying the user: judge, court, investigating judge **or public prosecutor**.
- **4(2)** — production order for traffic data (other than identification data) or content data:
  judge, court or investigating judge **only**.
- **4(3)** — **Preservation** Order for data of **any category**: judge, court, investigating judge
  **or public prosecutor**.

Read together: **a prosecutor may order content preserved but not produced.** The ladder is not a
single ranking of data sensitivity — it is sensitivity crossed with what the order actually does.

## 8. Three categories in one instrument, four in another

The Convention defines subscriber information negatively, as information about a subscriber "other
than traffic or content data" (Art. 18(3)). The Regulation defines four categories positively
(Arts. 3(9)–(12)), narrows subscriber data by an express carve-out for "passwords or other
authentication means", places "the location of the device" inside traffic data, and adds a category
the Convention has no counterpart for: **"data requested for the sole purpose of identifying the
user"** (Art. 3(10)).

That fourth category is not a label. It changes the authoriser (Art. 4(1) against 4(2)) and it is
carved out of the duty to notify the enforcing authority (Art. 8(1)). A reader working from a
three-category model gets all three of those wrong.

## 9. Asking a State and ordering a provider are different acts

Mutual legal assistance is addressed to a State, which then acts under its own law; a European
Production Order and a Protocol Article 7 order are addressed to a provider or its designated
establishment. The Union stated its own reason in Recital 8 of the Regulation: the European
Investigation Order and the Convention on Mutual Assistance in Criminal Matters both allow evidence
to be requested from another Member State, but "the procedures and timelines" they provide "might
not be appropriate for electronic evidence, which is more volatile and could more easily and
quickly be deleted".

The older route was **not** replaced. Article 27(1) of the Convention makes its own procedures apply
only "where there is no mutual assistance treaty or arrangement on the basis of uniform or
reciprocal legislation in force between the requesting and requested Parties" — a treaty-first
design, in which the Convention supplies a default rather than a replacement.

## 10. A rule about an obligation is not a rule about other legal systems

18 U.S.C. § 2713 attaches the preservation and disclosure obligation to what a provider has in its
"possession, custody, or control, regardless of whether such communication, record, or other
information is located within or outside of the United States". What that removes is a **location
argument against the obligation**. It does not state anything about whether another State's law
applies to the same data, and the wave says so in terms.

Regulation Art. 17(1) shows the other side of the same problem from inside an instrument: an
addressee that considers compliance would conflict with the applicable law of a third country may
inform the issuing and enforcing authorities by reasoned objection. Conflicting obligations are
anticipated by the instruments, not resolved by any one of them.

## What was deliberately not done

- **No ScheduledChange was added.** The schema requires `effectiveOn` to be an ISO date, and an
  active change must carry `certainty: 'enacted-with-date'`. There is no value for an enacted
  obligation with no date, and no way to omit the date — so the Article 19 / Article 25 trigger,
  whose date is genuinely not established, could not be represented honestly. All four candidates
  were rejected with reasons in `cross-border-digital-evidence-treaty-status.md`.
- **No provider is named anywhere in the wave**, and no ranking of providers or jurisdictions by
  compliance, resistance, disclosure, responsiveness or privacy appears. A test enforces both.
- **No response times, disclosure rates, request counts or success rates.** The wave's restricted
  quantitative claims stand at zero, which was the target.
- **Nothing operational.** No filing procedure, template, form, portal or contact channel; nothing
  about where data may be held, how a measure is executed, or how any process could be delayed,
  avoided or defeated.
