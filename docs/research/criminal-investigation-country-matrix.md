## B. Derived models

No system in this table is a pure type. The label records where responsibility formally sits.

| Model | Systems | What actually distinguishes it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Police-led, separate charging authority | Ireland, New Zealand, Kenya, United States | The police own the investigation; an independent prosecutor decides the charge |
| Police-led with a statutory prosecutorial direction power | **Kenya** | Art. 157(4) lets the DPP direct a *specific* investigation — a power Ireland's DPP is not claimed to have |
| Prosecution-responsible, police-executed | Germany | The prosecution carries the legal duty to investigate; the police carry their own statutory duty and do most of the work |
| Prosecutor-directed judicial police | France, Italy | The investigative function is legally exercised *under the direction of* a magistrate |
| Two-track: prosecutor enquiry + judge-led judicial investigation | Belgium, Portugal, Spain | A second, judge-conducted track exists for intrusive or serious matters |
| Police inquérito with exclusive prosecutorial action | Brazil | The police conduct a formal inquérito; the Ministério Público alone brings the action and exercises external control over police activity |
| Shared police / prosecutor investigation | Japan | Both may investigate; only the prosecutor charges |
| Fragmented by jurisdiction | United States | Competence follows the offence and the level, not a hierarchy |

## C. What the matrix forces

**1. The common-law / civil-law dichotomy fails on its own examples.**
Germany is an archetypal civil-law system whose investigation is not led by a judge. Japan applies
a civil-law-derived code with police doing the bulk of investigation. Kenya is a common-law system
whose prosecutor holds a constitutional power to direct a specific police investigation that
Ireland's prosecutor is not recorded as having. The families predict nothing here, and a page that
used them as an organising device would be wrong about at least four systems in this table.

**2. "Investigating judge" names two different offices.**
France, Belgium, Portugal and Spain have a judge who *conducts* an investigation. Germany's
§ 162 StPO office — which the Ministry's own English translation calls an *investigating judge* —
acts on the prosecution's application and examines whether the requested act is legally
permissible. Italy's giudice per le indagini preliminari likewise authorises and supervises rather
than directs. Same English label, opposite relationship to the investigation.

**3. "Judicial police" names at least three different things.**
In France it is a **legal function** exercised by designated personnel from more than one
institution. In Brazil it is an **institutional role** discharged by named police forces under a
delegado. In Italy it is **personnel drawn from** the national forces who fall under the direction
of the judicial authority. Reading any one of these as "police employed by the judiciary" is wrong
in every case.

**4. Directing an investigation and deciding the charge come apart almost everywhere.**
Of the systems here, the body that gathers the evidence is not the body that decides the charge in
Ireland, Kenya, Japan, Brazil, New Zealand, the United States and Argentina — and in France,
Germany and Italy the same magistracy holds both, which is a different design rather than an
absence of the safeguard. Neither arrangement is presented as better.

**5. Judicial authorisation is the one near-constant.**
Every system in the table places intrusive investigative measures behind an authorisation held by
someone outside the investigating team. What varies is who that person is and how early they
appear — not whether the gate exists.
