# Poland — model findings

What Poland added to the comparative picture, and what it reused.

## 1. The far end of the prosecution-independence spectrum — the same person is both offices

Batch B has now placed four countries on a spectrum of prosecution independence, and Poland sits at
the far executive end:

| country    | where the prosecution sits                                               |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Portugal   | constitutionally autonomous                                              |
| Italy      | inside the independent judiciary                                         |
| Austria    | a separate service, subordinate to the Minister of Justice               |
| Czechia    | part of the Ministry of Justice                                          |
| **Poland** | **the Prosecutor-General _is_ the Minister of Justice (personal union)** |

Poland is the sharpest case: not merely under the minister, or part of the ministry, but the same
office. This is stated from the statute (Law on the Prosecutor's Office 2016, Art. 1 § 2), and the
proposed separation is recorded — attributed and dated — as proposed, not enacted. Reuse of
`prosecutionScope: 'own'`; the distinctiveness is in the prose.

## 2. The neutrality discipline as a first-class deliverable

Poland is the batch's test of whether the honesty controls hold under political pressure. The
approach that worked:

- **Enacted vs. contested, kept apart.** The 2016 PG–MoJ union and the 2017 change to the KRS's
  judicial-member selection are _enacted facts_, stated with statutory citations. The _assessment_
  of them is _contested_, and is carried only by attribution to the CJEU (C-204/21, 5 June 2023) and
  the Commission's 2025 report — never in the site's voice.
- **A mechanical neutrality tripwire.** A test scans all reader-facing Poland prose and fails on a
  forbidden-terms list (captured, backsliding, authoritarian, party names, illiberal, politicised,
  purge, crisis, assault-on, court-packing). This makes the easy failure — sliding into advocacy —
  fail loudly, the same philosophy as the restricted-claims lexical layer.
- **Excluding what only news media reported.** Post-report developments in the dispute exist in the
  press but not in a primary/official source, so they are excluded rather than hedged.

This is the model finding that matters most: the framework's honesty controls generalise to a
politically contested subject without either flinching from the facts or editorialising about them.

## 3. Three separate top bodies, with an honest naming caveat

Poland has a Supreme Court, a separate supreme administrative court, and a separate Constitutional
Tribunal — the same multi-apex family as Austria and Czechia. One honesty wrinkle: the official
English translation of the Constitution renders the Naczelny Sąd Administracyjny as "Chief
Administrative Court", while its conventional English name is "Supreme Administrative Court". The
dossier shows both rather than silently picking one, because only the official-translation rendering
was actually verified.

## 4. Restricted claim: under capacity, with the rate deliberately withheld

Poland's SPACE I density is 85.7 — under capacity. The imprisonment rate behind it (about 201 per
100,000, the highest in Batch B) is deliberately kept _out_ of the restricted claim: a rate is an
invitation to the cross-country comparison SPACE I explicitly warns against, and a test asserts the
claim never smuggles a "per 100,000 / highest / compared" phrasing in. Reporting the density and
withholding the comparison is the honest handling.

## Net schema change

**None.** Poland reused unitary/national values, the multi-apex prose pattern, the
prosecution-scope value, and the enacted-vs-proposed discipline; its distinctiveness — and its
neutrality — live in prose and in the tests.
