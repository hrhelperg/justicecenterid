# Nigeria — model findings

What Nigeria added to the comparative picture, and what it reused.

## 1. The inverse federation — the batch's most distinctive model

Every other federation on the site puts police and prisons at the sub-national level: India, Mexico,
Argentina and the US all have states/provinces that run their own police and their own prisons.
Nigeria inverts that. Its **police and prisons are federal** (the Constitution provides a single
Nigeria Police Force and bars any other, and lists "Prisons" as a matter for the Federation alone),
while its **courts, prosecution and criminal law run at both levels**. It is modelled as a federal
record plus two illustrative state records (Kano, Lagos), whose scopes are the mirror image of
India's: courts, prosecution and legal-system `own`, but policing and corrections `none`. Placing
Nigeria beside India in the same programme makes the point sharp — "federation" does not fix which
functions decentralise.

## 2. Three criminal-law traditions under one apex

Because criminal law is residual to the states, Nigeria runs three substantive criminal-law
traditions simultaneously — the southern **Criminal Code**, the northern **Penal Code**, and the
**Sharia penal codes** adopted by twelve northern states from 1999–2001 (stated by dated attribution
to a 2004 Human Rights Watch report). All three sit under a single federal Constitution and a single
apex Supreme Court. This is the site's first case of multiple substantive criminal codes coexisting
within one country, and it is handled in prose — a criminal-law variant, not a separate jurisdiction
per code.

## 3. Prosecution inside the executive — a new pole on the prosecution axis

Nigeria's prosecutors are Cabinet ministers: the Attorney-General of the Federation is a Minister and
Chief Law Officer (s 150), and each state mirrors the office (s 195/211). Against the batch's
constitutionally independent prosecutions (Kenya's DPP, South Africa's single national authority) and
its middle cases, Nigeria marks the executive-embedded end of the axis — prosecution expressly inside
the political branch, restrained only by a duty to consider the public interest and prevent abuse of
process. Reused schema; the fact lives in prose.

## 4. A federally unified judiciary with diffuse review

Nigeria's states run their own superior courts (State High Courts, Sharia and Customary Courts of
Appeal), yet appointment, discipline and funding are unified under the federal National Judicial
Council and the apex is a single Supreme Court whose word is final (s 235). Constitutional review is
diffuse — any superior court of record, no separate constitutional court. That is a distinct shape:
state-run courts, but a nationally unified judiciary with diffuse review — modelled as `shared`
court scope on the federal record, unlike India's fully integrated single hierarchy (`own`).

## 5. Oversight anchored on a judicial council — and a pending reform

Oversight centres on the National Judicial Council (the body that unifies the two-tier judiciary),
with the National Human Rights Commission, the Public Complaints Commission ombudsman, and a
**split** police oversight (Police Service Commission + Nigeria Police Council; no single independent
police-complaints board). And Nigeria supplies the batch's clearest live-reform watch-flag: a
Constitution (Alteration) (State Police) Bill that, as of late July 2026, had passed both chambers
but was **not yet law** (attributed to the National Assembly Library Trust Fund). It is recorded in
prose as pending — not as a `ScheduledChange`, because it is not enacted with an effective date.

## Net schema change

**None.** Nigeria reused the federal + representative-state pattern (as India/Mexico/Argentina), but
with the scopes inverted (federal police/prisons, state courts/prosecution/law). The Sharia variant,
the executive prosecution, the diffuse review and the pending state-police reform all live in prose
and the tests.
