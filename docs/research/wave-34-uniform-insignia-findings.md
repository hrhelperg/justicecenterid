# Uniforms and insignia as institutional history — model findings

Research findings from Knowledge Expansion Wave 34. Three guides, four new sources, two services:
New Zealand Police and An Garda Síochána.

## The gap, and why the wave is small

Wave 26 published seven equipment guides and declared this gap in its own uncertainty:

> Uniform design, specification, colour schemes and insignia systems were NOT RESEARCHED for any
> country and are not described.

That sentence contains the difficulty. Most of what remains unresearched about equipment is
**specification-shaped** — placement, dimensions, protection levels, models — and specification is
exactly what this programme forbids publishing. The safe residue is history, and history is what
this wave published.

74 candidates were audited. 19 were already owned by Wave 26, 24 could not be sourced, 18 were
safety-rejected, and 10 were out of product scope.

## A service naming its own colonial origin

New Zealand Police do not soften where their rank insignia came from:

> The insignia of rank worn by New Zealand police officers have their origins in our colonial past.

The circumstances follow: in 1846 Governor George Grey "introduced a paramilitary force to carry out
both policing and military duties", and "The uniforms drew on the insignia of rank worn by the
British military."

**And it is not presented as finished.** The service says "There is still a strong parallel with
army ranks today" and publishes a comparison of the two systems, running from Commissioner to
Lieutenant General at one end and Constable to Private at the other.

Publishing that table is a choice. A service uneasy about the resemblance would describe its ranks
on their own terms; setting them beside army ranks treats the parallel as a fact about the
institution rather than an awkwardness.

The corpus records elsewhere that policing is a civil function distinguishable from military force.
A service whose visual language descends from an army carries a tension between those two things —
one its own history page states plainly.

## Uniform change is generational, and arrives attached to a finding

An Garda Síochána's new operational uniform became operational on 15 August 2022, and the service
frames it as rare:

> This change, during the 100th anniversary year of the founding of An Garda Síochána, is only the
> third time that An Garda Síochána has undertaken a formal change of the Garda uniform.

**Three formal changes in a century** puts a uniform in a different category from equipment replaced
on a cycle.

The drivers were institutional rather than aesthetic: a finding of the internal **Garda Cultural
Audit 2018**, which involved input from over 6,500 members, and a recommendation of the
**Commission on the Future of Policing in Ireland**. One is the organisation asking its own members;
the other is an external review of the whole of policing. A change resting on both had to survive
two quite different kinds of scrutiny.

Execution is a distribution problem: delivery to "approximately 13,000 members … in excess of 560
Garda stations across every Garda Region".

And the symbolic change is recorded separately from the practical one — "the first time that
elements of the uniform, other than the uniform cap, will feature the Garda Crest". A practical
redesign carried an identity decision, and the service treats them as different things.

## The year a uniform stopped differing by sex

New Zealand Police record the decision in their own words, dated **1994**:

> Clothing common to men and women to be identical. Women's skirts to be phased out as stocks used
> up. Women will have the choice of light or medium weight trousers or culottes.

Two things are worth drawing out.

**"Identical" is a stronger rule than "optional."** The difference is removed rather than made a
preference.

**"Phased out as stocks used up" is the sentence of an institution, not a manifesto.** A principle
and an inventory instruction in the same breath is what organisational change actually looks like
from inside.

It sits in a sequence: the original felt hat "was replaced by the forage cap in 1957", and a
policewomen's handbag was "issued from 1976 with the introduction of the vitric blue uniform".
Separate issue was the arrangement being changed — and reading the 1994 entry against those earlier
ones shows what was actually being ended.

Equipment decisions are rarely thought of as equality decisions, which is exactly why the record
matters: it dates the moment a daily statement was withdrawn.

## The line this wave would not cross

Every source sits beside material this platform must not publish. The Garda uniform and dress code
policy gives insignia placement, chevron positions and nameplate dimensions. **None of it is used.**

Wave 26 established that detail assisting imitation is deliberately absent, and the image policy
permits insignia only as documentation, never arranged so a page could be mistaken for an official
communication. What is published is when a uniform changed, why, what changing it took, and where
the visual system came from.

No supplier, manufacturer, model, specification, price or purchasing route appears anywhere. Cost
figures on a government procurement page were read and deliberately not used.

## Three claims that existed only in search summaries

Content-confirming each page rejected three plausible facts that were not on the pages: a 1995 move
of rank designations from sleeve to epaulette; the ranks the force held at its founding; and a 1977
trial of trousers for policewomen. Each is recorded in the relevant source note as **DOES NOT
SUPPORT**, so the next editor meeting the same summary knows it was already checked.
