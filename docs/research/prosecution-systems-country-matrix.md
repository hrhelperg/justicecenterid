# Wave 10 — comparative prosecution-system matrix

Rows are systems. Columns are structural questions, not model names. Values are `YES`, `NO`,
`PARTIAL`, `CONTESTED` and `NOT ESTABLISHED`.

`NOT ESTABLISHED` is never rendered as `NO`. Several dossiers record appointment relationships and
independence-in-practice as unresearched; those cells stay open, and so do the pages.

**No universal charging test exists in this evidence.** The phrase "realistic prospect of
conviction" appears nowhere in the sources for any of these eight systems, and Germany's statute
asks a different question entirely.

## D. What the matrix forces

**1. There is no universal charging standard, and the site previously implied one.**
Germany's § 152(2) makes prosecution a **duty** triggered by sufficient factual indications; the
United States gives local prosecutors broad discretion with no national standard. A page saying
prosecution services "typically" apply an evidential test asking about prospects of conviction
describes neither, and rests on no country-scoped source.

**2. The common-law/civil-law binary fails immediately.**
Germany is the archetypal legality system — and § 153 StPO gives its prosecutors a statutory power
to refrain from prosecuting minor matters where there is no public interest, which is a
discretionary judgement inside a mandatory framework. Meanwhile Kenya, a common-law system, gives
its DPP a constitutional insulation stronger than anything in the German or French texts. Neither
family predicts the answer.

**3. Hierarchy is not political control, and the two must be stated separately.**
Spain's Ministerio Fiscal is constitutionally bound to _dependencia jerárquica_. France's parquet
is hierarchical and acts under the minister's authority — while the same source says instructions
may never be given in individual files. Germany allocates supervision by statute and, because the
courts are Land courts, **no single ministry supervises prosecution across the country at all**.
Hierarchy describes a chain of supervision. Whether it is used improperly is a different claim,
and one these sources do not make.

**4. "Independent prosecution service" is not one thing.**
Kenya's is a constitutional prohibition on direction. Brazil's is functional independence for an
institution outside all three branches. Ireland's is the office's own statement. Spain's
constitution says the opposite, and says it as a principle rather than a defect. Applying one
country's vocabulary to another is the central terminology error available in this material.

**5. Prosecution is not always a distinct service at all.**
In Nigeria the prosecuting authority is a serving Minister. In the United States there is no
national prosecution service and most chief local prosecutors are elected. In Germany the offices
attach to courts rather than forming a service. The Anglo-Irish model of a single independent
prosecuting office is one arrangement among several, not the default.

**6. Brazil does not fit the category being compared.**
The Ministério Público holds the public criminal action exclusively **and** conducts civil
proceedings for diffuse and collective interests, and exercises external control over police
activity. Describing it as Brazil's prosecution service is true and radically incomplete.
