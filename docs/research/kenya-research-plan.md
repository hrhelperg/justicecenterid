# Kenya — research plan

Kenya is country 9→10 of Batch C (the tenth is Nigeria). It is the batch's second demonstration —
after South Africa — that a country can decentralise heavily and still run justice entirely at the
national level, and it adds a distinctive prosecution–police relationship.

## Questions the pilot had to answer

1. **Constitutional supremacy or parliamentary sovereignty?** — supremacy: the 2010 Constitution is
   the supreme law and any inconsistent law is void (Article 2), so the courts can strike down
   statutes. Common-law tradition. The opposite of New Zealand.
2. **Federal or devolved, and which tier runs what?** — a devolved **unitary** state with 47 county
   governments, but justice is national. The Fourth Schedule Part 1 assigns courts (item 8) and
   police/criminal law/correctional services (item 7) to the national government; prosecution is the
   national ODPP (Article 157); the counties run none of the four functions. → one country record,
   no sub-national record.
3. **What is the apex and the review model?** — the Supreme Court (created by the 2010 Constitution)
   is the apex and binds every other court (Article 163(7)); constitutional review is **diffuse** —
   the High Court determines whether a law is inconsistent with the Constitution (Article 165(3)(d))
   — with **no separate constitutional court**.
4. **Where does prosecution sit?** — a constitutionally **independent** Director of Public
   Prosecutions (Article 157), de-linked from the Attorney-General (Article 156), not under the
   direction or control of any authority (157(10)), single non-renewable eight-year term (157(5)),
   and — distinctively — able to **direct the Inspector-General to investigate** (157(4)).
5. **Police and prisons?** — one National Police Service functioning throughout Kenya (Article 243),
   two services, an Inspector-General with independent command (245(2)(b)) and an **investigation
   firewall** (245(4)); a national Kenya Prisons Service under the State Department for Correctional
   Services.
6. **Oversight?** — rich: the Article 59 commissions (KNCHR, NGEC, and the CAJ/Ombudsman), the
   Judicial Service Commission (Articles 171–172), the statutory Independent Policing Oversight
   Authority, and the National Police Service Commission (Article 246).
7. **Prisons and numbers?** — World Prison Brief: population 60,740 (average, 2025) at a rate of 101;
   capacity 34,000 and occupancy ~176.5%, both dated December 2023 — reported with the two dates kept
   distinct.

## Why no sub-national records (the contrast that matters)

Kenya joins South Africa as the batch's demonstration that heavy decentralisation does **not**
automatically mean sub-national justice. Mexico, Argentina and India each earned sub-national records
because their states/provinces run their own courts, police and prisons. Kenya's counties run
**none** of the four functions — the Fourth Schedule makes this exact — so a single country record is
the honest model.

## Honesty points

- The 2024 protests and their policing, the 2022 BBI litigation and the 2023 police-reform taskforce
  are left out (contested, unresolved or non-structural).
- Current office-holders are not named; one reported surname spelling could not be resolved.
- IPOA and the Article 59 commissions are described at the level of the body and its function; their
  exact statutory Acts were not re-fetched line by line.

## Sources targeted

The 2010 Constitution (full Kenya Law text, including the Fourth Schedule), the ODPP, and the World
Prison Brief.

## Model discipline

No new schema; one country-level record. Kenya's distinctive facts — diffuse review with a 2010 apex
Supreme Court, the independent DPP that can direct an investigation, the police firewall, and the
Kadhis' courts — are carried in prose and the tests.
