# Police equipment — model findings

Wave 26. Five institutional sources, content-confirmed on 6 September 2026. Every statement is about
what equipment exists, who issues it, under what policy or standard, and how widely it is used.
**Nothing here describes how anything is used, carried, configured, selected or bought.**

## 1. Published equipment lists do not look like the popular picture

An Garda Síochána publishes what a member takes on duty, in its own Uniform and Dress Code policy:

> "A member going on duty will carry the appropriate PPE, in the official utility belt, and pouches
> issued including extendible baton, handcuffs, incapacitant spray, anti-spit guard (where
> applicable), a notebook, pen, medi-pouch and general purpose safety helmet."

A notebook, a pen and a medical pouch sit in the same sentence as the protective and restraint
items, in a single list with no hierarchy. That is a police service saying, in its own governance
document, that recording what happened and treating an injury are equipment questions of the same
order as everything else.

It is the same imbalance Wave 24 found in training: measured academy instruction gives more hours to
law than to firearms or defensive tactics. **What a service issues, like what it teaches, is a
better guide to the job than what fiction selects from it.**

## 2. The uniform is equipment, and its first function is not authority

The Dutch national police state the ordering themselves:

> "Zichtbaarheid en herkenbaarheid zijn daarmee de belangrijkste functies van het uniform."

Visibility and recognisability first. Authority second — "Het uniform 'zegt' ook dat de politieagent
de autoriteit is op straat" — and protection third: "Daarnaast biedt het uniform ook veiligheid en
bescherming."

The common assumption reverses the first two. And the three functions can pull against each other —
maximum visibility, unmistakable authority and physical protection are not automatically the same
garment, which is why An Garda Síochána resolves it with **three uniform categories**: Operational,
Dress and Formal.

The Garda policy also places the **Personal Protective Vest inside the Operational Uniform** rather
than beside it, which is a statement about how routine the category has become.

## 3. Recognisability is an accountability function, not a convenience

It works in both directions: it lets a person in trouble find help, and it lets a person who was
dealt with badly say who dealt with them. The second is what every complaints mechanism in the
corpus quietly assumes.

## 4. The identification duty is asymmetric, and the asymmetry is deliberate

The Dutch rule differs by dress:

> "Een politiemedewerker in uniform hoeft zich niet uit zichzelf te legitimeren maar als u ernaar
> vraagt, moet het politielegitimatiebewijs wel getoond worden. Een politiemedewerker in
> 'burgerkleding' moet wel uit eigen beweging het politielegitimatiebewijs tonen."

A uniformed officer need not identify themselves unprompted but must show the card if asked; an
officer in plain clothes must show it on their own initiative.

The logic is exact: a uniform has already answered the question, so the document is a backstop.
Plain clothes answers nothing, so the document becomes the whole answer — and the duty moves
accordingly.

## 5. Equipment governance is a lifecycle, and disposal is in it for a reason

The Garda policy defines uniform as items "issued to Garda Personnel to wear for the purpose of
carrying out their duties" — framed around issue, not ownership — and carries sections on issue and
replacement, on **disposal**, and on issue to student and probationer Gardaí.

Disposal is the revealing one. A uniform that leaves a service without being disposed of is a
uniform someone else can wear, so governing the end of the lifecycle is the same problem as
identification at the start of it.

The standards also apply to "uniformed and plain-clothes members, and Garda staff" — being out of
uniform does not put a member outside equipment governance.

## 6. Equipment standards solve a problem the buyer cannot solve

A police service buying protective equipment cannot verify it, and finds out whether it works only
when it matters. The United States answer:

- NIJ "has been setting voluntary body armor standards since 1972".
- "the NIJ standard is the only nationally accepted standard for the body armor worn by law
  enforcement and corrections officers".
- NIJ "administers a program to test commercially available armor for compliance".
- A compliant manufacturer "is subject to six follow-up inspections and testing over a five-year
  period".

**Two findings.** First, the standards are _voluntary_ — the pressure runs through procurement
rather than through law, which works only while buyers care what they buy. Second, the follow-up
regime is what makes it mean anything: certifying a submitted sample proves something about that
sample; re-testing recently manufactured items proves something about what is actually sold.

This is structurally the same arrangement as forensic accreditation, where a court cannot verify a
laboratory's competence itself. **Independent verification of a claim the buyer cannot check is a
recurring institutional pattern, not a feature of one field.**

## 7. Body-worn video: adoption is not the same as governance

Bureau of Justice Statistics, local police departments in the United States:

|                                     | 2016  | 2020       |
| ----------------------------------- | ----- | ---------- |
| All department sizes                | 43.1% | **61.8%**  |
| Serving 1 million or more residents | 73.3% | **100.0%** |

Complete adoption in the largest departments while the overall figure stayed under two-thirds. So
**"do police wear cameras" has no single answer even inside one country** — the same decentralisation
the recruitment layer found, showing up in equipment.

The same survey treats body-worn cameras as a subject of **written policy**, alongside use-of-force
reporting and vehicle pursuits. Adoption and governance are separate facts, which is why it asks
about both — and a department with cameras and no policy has bought hardware rather than adopted an
accountability instrument.

## 8. Why the equipment arrived at all

Policing had a recording problem long before cameras: most decisions are taken without a supervisor
present and reviewed afterwards through an account written by the person being reviewed. Body-worn
video is an institutional answer to that, which is why it spread fastest where scrutiny is greatest.

It does not replace the written record. Documentation equipment remains on issue lists, and a
recording captures what a camera saw rather than what an officer knew, decided or was told.

The Garda document is the anchor because it is an official, openly published policy marked
**NEAMHSHRIANTA / UNRESTRICTED**, version 1.2, effective 24 January 2025, approved by the Garda
Executive. It settles what a police service says its own uniform is for without anyone inferring it.

## What was deliberately not published

**No product, brand, manufacturer, model, supplier, price, specification, protection level,
capability figure or purchasing guidance of any kind** — for unregulated categories as much as for
regulated ones.

Restricted categories are **named where a service's own policy names them** and explained nowhere:
the pages record that batons, handcuffs and incapacitant spray are issued, and route the question of
when force may lawfully be used to the existing legal page.

Also absent: how any item is used, carried or configured; what identity-document security features
look like or how to check them; what any device can capture; and how any service procures.
