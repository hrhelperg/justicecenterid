# Lay participation in judging — model findings

Research findings from Knowledge Expansion Wave 30. Four guides, three new sources, two reused,
three systems: Germany, Japan, and England and Wales, with the United States cited for the
constitutional entrenchment of trial by jury.

## The gap this closes

The corpus leaned on this concept in sixteen files before it had a page for it:

- the `/courts` section's own `variationNote` names "the use of juries or lay judges" as a principal
  axis of variation between legal traditions;
- the Austria dossier states that lay participation is constitutionally required;
- the Japan dossier has to tell readers that the saiban-in system is "not a jury", and that where
  "jury" is used it is unsuitable;
- the United States dossier and `/courts/why-hearings-are-public` both quote the Sixth Amendment.

Fifty-three sentences mentioned a jury or a juror. The glossary held one passing use of "jury" and
no entry at all for lay judge, assessor, Schöffe or saiban-in.

## "Jury" is not the general word

This is the finding that shapes every page in the cluster. There are two families:

|                           | Separate lay body                               | Mixed panel                                        |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Who sits                  | Lay people alone                                | Lay members and professional judges together       |
| Relationship to the judge | Reaches its own conclusion apart from the judge | One bench, deciding together                       |
| Examples researched       | The jury, entrenched in the Sixth Amendment     | Schöffengericht (Germany), saiban-in panel (Japan) |

Japan's own Supreme Court is careful here, describing its arrangement as having points in common
with a jury system rather than as being one. Reading "jury" into a mixed panel produces the wrong
picture of who sits with whom, so every page uses "lay participation" as the general term and
reserves "jury" for the specific thing.

## Both mixed systems put lay members in a two-to-one majority

| System                   | Composition                                                                                        |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Germany, Schöffengericht | "Das Schöffengericht besteht aus dem Richter beim Amtsgericht als Vorsitzenden und zwei Schöffen." |
| Japan, saiban-in panel   | "consists of three judges and six saiban-in"                                                       |
| Japan, reduced panel     | "one judge and four saiban-in"                                                                     |

Two panels differing in size by a factor of three land on exactly the same proportion, and the
reduced Japanese panel goes further still at one to four. Whatever else these systems disagree
about, they agree that lay members should outnumber professionals.

Germany also permits a second professional judge to be added on the prosecution's application when
the main proceedings are opened — which is a provision allowing the balance itself to be altered.

## Neither system confines lay members to the verdict

This is where the popular model of lay participation is simply wrong about both systems researched.

**Germany does not divide the office.** During the main hearing, lay judges exercise "das Richteramt
in vollem Umfang und mit gleichem Stimmrecht wie die Richter" — the judicial office in full, with
the same voting right as the professional judges.

**And it bounds that in time rather than by subject.** Decisions required outside the main hearing
are taken by the professional judge alone. The lay judges are full members of a court that exists
only while it is sitting.

**Japan names sentencing explicitly.** Saiban-in participate in decisions on "fact finding;
application of laws and regulations; and sentencing". Fact-finding is what a jury does; the other
two are not, in the systems where juries sit. Sentencing is the sharpest test of the design, because
it is the decision that determines what happens to a person.

## Eligibility ties lay service to the franchise

England and Wales states the qualification in one place: a person is qualified if "registered as a
parliamentary or local government elector and aged eighteen or over but under seventy six", and
"ordinarily resident in the United Kingdom, the Channel Islands or the Isle of Man for any period of
at least five years since attaining the age of thirteen".

Three things are worth drawing out.

- **It runs through the electoral register**, which makes jury service an incident of the franchise.
  Whoever is missing from the roll is missing from the jury, and the two questions stop being
  separable.
- **It is bounded at both ends.** Voting has a lower age limit and no upper one; this has both.
- **The residence rule is historical, not current.** Five years at any time since the age of
  thirteen — a rule about attachment to the place rather than presence in it.

One test governs service in the Crown Court, the High Court and the county court, so the standard
does not vary with what is at stake.

## What is deliberately absent

**No jury size appears anywhere in the cluster.** It is the fact a reader most expects, and the
section of the Juries Act read here governs qualification rather than size. No source establishing a
number was obtained, and a number nobody verified is worse than an absence that says so. A test
enforces this, and another requires the cluster to say why the number is missing.

**Austria is described in no new guide.** Its constitution requires lay participation, but
`ris.bka.gv.at` returned HTTP 503 to every automated request across three paths. That is an access
limitation and never evidence about Austrian arrangements.

**The disqualifications are not described.** They sit in a Schedule that was not read, so the
eligibility page describes who is qualified and says plainly that it does not describe who is not.
