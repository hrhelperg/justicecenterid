# Wave 21 cannibalization matrix — 47 candidates, 12 published

Part F makes this the first publication gate, and in this wave it is the binding one. Every
candidate below was compared against all 121 published guides, not against a section. The
question asked of each was the brief's: **does this answer a genuinely different reader
question?** Where it did not, the candidate was merged or rejected, never renamed.

Verdicts: **PUBLISH** · **MERGE** (its content belongs inside another Wave 21 page) · **ALIAS**
(same question, different words) · **DEFER** (real gap, insufficient evidence this wave) ·
**REJECT** (the corpus already owns it).

## The rejection that shaped the wave

`search-and-seizure` was the single most obvious candidate in the brief's own example list, and
rejecting the **name** while publishing the **subject** is what forced the wave's central finding.
Once the page could not be called "search and seizure", it had to be decided what it was about —
and the answer turned out to differ by system. South Africa and Kenya reach it through privacy and
never mention a warrant. Japan and the United States state a warrant rule and never mention
privacy. Splitting it into a home page and a communications page followed from the evidence, not
from a content plan: the Netherlands requires a judge for one and not the other, and Germany
requires a judge for the other and not, in the same way, the one.
