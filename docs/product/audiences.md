# Audiences

Audiences are listed in priority order. Priority determines what we build first and whose
comprehension we optimise for when two audiences conflict.

## P1 — The general international reader

**Who.** An adult anywhere in the world who has encountered a term, a role, or an
institution and wants to understand it. Often a second-language English reader. Usually
arriving from search with a single specific question.

**What they need.** A direct answer in the first paragraph. A definition before any history.
An explicit statement that arrangements differ by country, so they do not carry a
US-television assumption into their own jurisdiction.

**What breaks for them.** Untranslated jurisdictional jargon; pages that open with etymology
or Roman law; long unbroken prose; the assumption that "the police" is a single kind of
organisation.

**Design consequences.** Every guide opens with a one-paragraph plain-language definition
before anything else. Reading level target B2. Glossary terms are defined inline on first
use, not only linked.

## P2 — Students and educators

**Who.** Secondary and undergraduate students in law, criminology, political science,
history, and public administration; the teachers preparing their materials.

**What they need.** Structure they can reuse — clear taxonomies, comparative framing, and
above all _citable primary sources_. A teacher will not assign a page that does not show
where its claims came from.

**What breaks for them.** Unsourced assertions; secondary sources presented as primary; no
indication of when a claim was last checked; no way to tell analysis from fact.

**Design consequences.** Visible source lists with type and date on every substantive page.
Explicit fact-versus-analysis labelling. Review metadata rendered, not just stored.

## P3 — Journalists, researchers, and analysts

**Who.** Reporters covering a justice story outside their home jurisdiction; NGO and
policy researchers; comparative-law researchers doing an initial survey.

**What they need.** Fast orientation in an unfamiliar system, correct institutional names,
and a reliable jumping-off point to primary material. They need to know what our coverage
does _not_ include as much as what it does.

**What breaks for them.** False equivalence between institutions across systems; stale
structural descriptions presented as current; hidden coverage gaps.

**Design consequences.** Coverage status is public. Historical structures are labelled
historical with the period attached. Equivalence between institutions is stated as a claim
with a source, never assumed.

## P4 — People considering or entering public-service careers

**Who.** Prospective and early-career officers, investigators, forensic staff, court staff,
corrections and probation officers, dispatchers, border and customs officers.

**What they need.** An honest account of what a role involves: responsibilities, training
routes, the constraints and accountability that come with authority, and the real pressures
and risks of the work.

**What breaks for them.** Recruitment-brochure tone; fabricated salary figures; national
requirements presented as universal; either romanticising or denigrating the work.

**Design consequences.** Profession pages describe responsibilities, decision authority,
constraints, oversight, and typical training _structures_ — never invented pay, mortality,
staffing, or entry statistics. Country-specific requirements appear only under a researched
country entity.

## P5 — Institutional and civic-education users

**Who.** Public-legal-education programmes, libraries, museums, civic-integration and
newcomer-orientation programmes, and institutions producing their own explanatory material.

**What they need.** Neutral, reusable explanations that carry no political framing and can be
placed in front of a mixed audience without endorsement risk.

**Design consequences.** Strict political neutrality. No country is used as the implicit
default or the implicit cautionary example.

## Audiences we do not serve

Being explicit about this prevents scope drift and shapes what we refuse to publish.

- **People seeking advice on their own legal situation.** We describe systems, never
  situations. Every relevant page carries the not-legal-advice statement and directs the
  reader to qualified local professionals or the responsible official authority.
- **People in an emergency.** We never publish emergency instructions. The only appropriate
  response is to direct the reader to their local emergency services.
- **Anyone seeking operational tradecraft.** Nothing is written to help a reader evade
  detection or investigation, defeat a security or forensic control, conduct surveillance on
  another person, obtain restricted capability, or interfere with a proceeding, a witness, or
  an officer. This constrains how investigative and forensic topics are written: we explain
  _what a discipline establishes and what its limits are_, not how to perform or defeat it.
- **Partisan campaigners.** We will not be a citation source for political messaging, and we
  write so that we are equally unusable by all of them.

## Conflict resolution

When audience needs conflict, resolve in this order:

1. **Safety** — the content-safety rules override every other consideration.
2. **Accuracy** — never simplify to the point of being wrong. If a concept cannot be made
   accurate at B2 level, keep it accurate and add a plain-language summary above it.
3. **P1 comprehension** — the general reader's ability to follow the page.
4. **P2/P3 citability** — depth, sourcing, and structural completeness.

The standard resolution of (3) against (4) is layering, not compromise: plain-language
summary first, structured detail second, sources last. The general reader stops early; the
researcher scrolls.
